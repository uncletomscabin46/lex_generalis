From Idea to Investible Company: Part Four – The Cap Table

What It Is, Why It Matters, and How to Keep It Clean

In Part 3 of this series, we discussed how early investment works, SAFEs, convertible notes, and why documenting early money properly is so important. In this piece, we are going to look more closely at the cap table. If the SAFE is the instrument you use to bring in early money, the cap table is the record that tracks where all of that ownership ends up. The earlier you start taking it seriously, the better.

What exactly is a cap table?

Cap table is short for capitalization table. It is a record of everyone who owns a piece of your company and how much they own. At the very beginning, it is simple. You incorporated the company, you issued shares to yourself, and you are the only entry on the cap table.

But it grows quickly. If you bring on a co-founder, they get shares. If you hire an early employee and give them equity, they go on the cap table. If a friend invests through a SAFE, that SAFE goes on the cap table. When you raise a seed round and investors receive preferred stock, they go on the cap table too. Every layer of ownership the company has ever issued is reflected there.

The cap table, at its core, is a snapshot of the company's ownership at any given moment. It tells you what percentage of the company each person or entity owns, what type of equity they hold, and what terms are attached to it.

Why founder vesting matters

One of the first things you should put in place once you have incorporated is a vesting schedule for founders. Vesting is the process by which founders earn their equity over time, rather than owning it all outright from day one.

A very common structure is a four-year vesting schedule with a one-year cliff. Here is what that means in practice. If you issue yourself shares under this arrangement, you will not own any of them outright until you have been with the company for one full year. At the one-year mark, a quarter of your shares vest all at once. After that, the remaining shares vest gradually, usually on a monthly basis, over the next three years.

Why does this matter? Because startups do not always go the way you planned. Sometimes a co-founder leaves early. Sometimes the relationship breaks down. Sometimes the person who seemed essential at the start simply moves on. Without a vesting schedule, that person walks away with a large stake in the company even though they did not see the journey through. Vesting protects the company, the remaining founders, and ultimately the investors who will want to know that the people driving the business actually have skin in the game.

The option pool

In part two of this series, we talked about the equity incentive plan, which is the pool of shares set aside to compensate employees, advisors, and other service providers with equity. On the cap table, this pool shows up as a reserved allocation before it is actually used. Investors will often expect to see an option pool of around ten to fifteen percent of the company's shares at the time of a seed round.

One thing to understand about option pools is that they dilute existing shareholders. When you create the pool, you are setting aside shares that reduce everyone else's percentage of ownership in the company, which is normal and expected. The key is to be deliberate about the size of the pool and to understand how it interacts with your overall ownership structure before you sit down with a professional investor.

What investors look at

When a professional investor looks at your company, one of the first things they will ask for is the cap table. What they are trying to understand is who owns the company, how clean the ownership history is, and whether there are any surprises that could complicate an investment.

They will want to see that founders are properly vested, that early SAFEs and convertible notes are documented and accounted for, that the option pool is in place, and that no one has been issued equity through a handshake agreement that was never formalized. A messy cap table does not automatically kill a deal, but it does create friction, slow things down, and sometimes raise questions that are hard to answer cleanly.

The good news is that keeping a clean cap table is not complicated if you start early. There are tools built specifically for this purpose. Carta is one of the most widely used platforms for cap table management and is free for early-stage companies. Using a proper tool, rather than a spreadsheet, means your records are organized, your documents are in one place, and you can pull a clean cap table report any time someone asks for it.

The bottom line

The cap table is not just an administrative document. It is a reflection of the promises you have made, the risks people have taken on you, and the ownership structure you have built over time. Keeping it accurate and clean from the very beginning is one of the simplest things you can do to make your company more credible and more investible.
